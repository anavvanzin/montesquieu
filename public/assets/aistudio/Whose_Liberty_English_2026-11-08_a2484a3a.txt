Whose liberty? Montesquieu in Brazil
Ana Vitória Vanzin Mendes | 11 November 2026
English discussant script

[Slide 1 · Whose liberty? Montesquieu in Brazil]

Thank you, Serge, and thank you, Arno.

I would like to approach Montesquieu through a question: whose liberty does a legal order sustain?

The Brazilian Civil Code of 1916 gives that question a concrete form. Article 6 placed married women among those considered relatively incapable of performing certain civil acts. I want to examine the reasoning behind that classification, alongside two images of public authority.

My argument concerns the work done by context. Attention to a society’s particular circumstances can help explain the conditions of liberty. But an appeal to those circumstances can also justify restrictions. The relationship between those two uses of context is the thread I would like to follow.

[Slide 2 · Liberty and its conditions]

Montesquieu’s separation of powers is familiar. Less easily captured in a diagram is the relationship between laws and the social conditions in which they operate. In The Spirit of the Laws, laws are considered in relation to customs, religion, commerce, and particular histories.

The position of women brings this relationship into focus. In Book Seven, chapter nine, Montesquieu writes that women in republics are “free by the laws, and held captive by mores”. This is part of his comparison of political regimes, rather than a general declaration of women’s equality.

I take the passage as a point of departure for a historical question. How do legal rules and social expectations work together to distribute freedom? If we ask how public power is limited, we should also ask how authority within the household is organised.

Those questions are connected, but their connection has to be established in particular historical settings.

[Slide 3 · Reception through adaptation]

The Brazilian Constitution of 1824 offers a useful reminder that reception involves choices. Article 10 recognised four powers: legislative, moderating, executive, and judicial.

The difference from the familiar three-part scheme invites us to investigate what constitutional actors selected and changed. The circulation of a book, the use of an argument, and the construction of an institution are distinct historical events. Evidence for one does not automatically establish the others.

I would apply the same discipline to images. A familiar symbol can travel between settings. To connect it to Montesquieu, however, we need more than a resemblance. The images I will show open questions about legal authority; they do not prove a direct line of influence.

[Slide 4 · Who participates in the constitutional relationship?]

Aurélio de Figueiredo painted Compromisso Constitucional in 1896. It represents Deodoro’s constitutional oath in February 1891. The interval matters: this is a retrospective representation of a constitutional moment.

[Pause for five seconds.]

Deodoro holds a book before the assembly, with Prudente de Moraes at the centre. Article 44 of the 1891 Constitution prescribed the presidential commitment. I read the scene as presenting public authority through an obligation.

Now look at the gallery. Women watch while men enact the constitutional commitment on the floor. This is an observation about the composition, rather than a claim about everyone who attended the event or the painter’s documented intention.

The contrast makes participation a question we can ask of the image. Who appears as a party to the constitutional relationship, and who appears as its spectator? The painting does not answer how women’s legal position was organised. For that, we have to bring the image into conversation with other sources.

[Slide 5 · A feminine figure of justice]

Ceschiatti’s A Justiça, outside the Supreme Federal Court, offers a different relationship between women and authority. Here, a woman’s figure represents justice itself. The sword rests across her lap.

[Pause for five seconds.]

I read the stillness as force held in reserve. Whether the sword also expresses judicial independence needs evidence about its historical use and institutional setting.

The sculpture dates from 1961. The following year, the Married Woman’s Statute removed married women from article 6’s list of the relatively incapable. Yet the reform retained the husband as head of the conjugal society, now exercising that function with his wife’s collaboration.

The juxtaposition is mine. I am not attributing a commentary on the Code to Ceschiatti. What interests me is the coexistence of a feminine figure of institutional authority and restrictions on actual women’s legal capacity. Representing justice as a woman did not settle the question of women’s position before the law.

[Slide 6 · The limits of legal capacity]

The drafting history of article 6 helps us make that question more precise.

Beviláqua’s primitive project did not include married women in its formal list of the relatively incapable. The next article nevertheless restricted their civil capacity under their husbands’ direction. The project also already made the husband head of the conjugal society.

On 16 April 1900, the Revising Commission moved the married-women provision into the incapacity list. In October 1901, Beviláqua defended his earlier distinction, while continuing to justify restrictions through the direction, unity, and harmony of the family.

The distinction matters: restrictions preceded the classification. We cannot describe this as an egalitarian project to which legislators simply added marital hierarchy.

Beviláqua’s accompanying justification described marriage as an egalitarian society under the husband’s direction. It expressly invoked Spencer in support of male headship. The tension is within the argument itself: a claim to extend rights and freedom coexists with a defence of hierarchy.

This gives us a documented use of social organisation to justify unequal capacity. It does not establish that Beviláqua derived the provision from Montesquieu. My comparison concerns how an argument about the conditions of a society can support a legal restriction. A genealogy of that argument would require further evidence.

[Slide 7 · Questions for discussion]

I would therefore leave two questions for discussion.

When a legal rule travels, what happens to the arguments used to justify it? A history of reception should follow those arguments as closely as it follows the wording of the rule. In this case, the organisation of the family supplied a reason for restricting women’s civil activity.

And whose liberty does the resulting order sustain? The women in the painted gallery and the feminine Justice before the Court give us different points of entry into that question. Neither image resolves it alone.

My proposal is to read representations alongside the legal texts and institutional records. Together, they allow us to investigate how authority is made visible, how capacity is distributed, and how those arrangements are justified. That is the connection between Montesquieu, the Brazilian example, and legal iconography that I would like to discuss. Thank you.
